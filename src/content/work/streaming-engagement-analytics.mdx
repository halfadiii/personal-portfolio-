## The short version

Netflix publishes what people watch, in two separate reports that don't line
up. I joined them to answer a simple question: what's actually driving the
hours?

The answer: people are watching more, but only because there's more to watch.
The average title gets fewer hours than it used to.

## Where the data comes from

Twice a year, Netflix lists every title and the hours it was watched. That
covers about 99% of all viewing, but with no countries and no week-by-week
detail.

Every week, it also publishes a Top 10 for the world and for 94 countries. That
has the weeks and the countries, but only ten titles at a time. And for
countries it's only a ranking, with no hours.

## How it works

1. **Keep the two reports separate.** Squashing them into one table would mean
   inventing numbers that one of them doesn't have.
2. **Make the titles match.** The same show is written three different ways
   across the files, so those had to be lined up first.
3. **Check it against Netflix.** Before analysing anything, my totals had to
   match the ones Netflix published: 95.19, 96.21 and 97.66 billion hours.

## What it found

- **More titles, not bigger hits.** Total hours went up each half-year, while
  the average title fell from 5.88 million hours to 5.62 million.
- **Series beat films, and the gap is growing.** A series earns about three
  and a half times what a film does. Films grew in number and still lost
  hours.
- **Non-English films chart more and get watched less.** More of them reach
  the global Top 10 than English films, and they earn less than half the
  hours.
- **A new season brings the old one back.** When *The Gentlemen* returned, its
  first season, off the global chart since May 2024, came back into the Top 10
  of 56 countries that week and 85 the week after.
- **Countries hold on to hits for very different lengths of time.** A title
  stays in Ukraine's Top 10 for 5.8 weeks on average, and in Trinidad and
  Tobago's for 2.2.

## What it can't tell you

Netflix doesn't publish hours by country, or genres. So anything about
countries here is based on rankings, and I can tell a series from a film but
not a comedy from a drama.

Two mistakes in my own queries were caught before they reached a chart. One
merged two seasons of the same show into one. The other returned nothing at
all, which was lucky: a slightly milder version of that mistake returns a
number that looks fine.

The dashboard is on this site. The Power BI version is written up with the
code and not published yet.
